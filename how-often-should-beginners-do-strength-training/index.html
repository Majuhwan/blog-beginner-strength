How Often Should Beginners Do Strength Training

What CDC and WHO physical activity guidelines actually recommend for muscle-strengthening frequency, and how to apply that as a true beginner.

Beginner StrengthOct 9, 2026 · 3 min read

This article is informational only and is not medical advice. Talk to a doctor before starting a new exercise program, especially if you have an existing health condition.

One of the most common questions from people starting strength training is simple: how many days a week should I actually be lifting? The answer from major public health guidelines is more specific, and more modest, than the exercise industry often suggests.

What the guidelines actually say

The World Health Organization's physical activity fact sheet states that adults should do muscle-strengthening activities at a moderate or greater intensity on at least 2 days per week, in addition to meeting aerobic activity recommendations. The United States CDC's physical activity guidance for adults aligns with this same baseline: at least 2 days per week of activities that work all major muscle groups, including legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders, and arms.

Notice what this guidance does not say. It does not say 5 or 6 days a week. It does not specify a particular program, split, or number of sets. The floor set by both WHO and CDC is 2 days per week, which is a far lower bar than many beginner workout plans assume by default.

Why "at least" matters

Both organizations frame this as a minimum, not a target to stay at forever. The WHO guidelines also note that muscle-strengthening activity benefits everyone, across all age groups, which is part of why it appears as a standing recommendation rather than something aimed only at athletes or people trying to build visible muscle. For a true beginner, meeting that 2-day minimum consistently, for weeks in a row, is a more realistic and sustainable goal than jumping into a 5-day program that leads to burnout or missed sessions within the first month.

What counts as "all major muscle groups"

The CDC's description specifies working your legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders, and arms. This doesn't require a separate exercise for each muscle group in every session. Many effective beginner routines use compound movements, exercises that work several muscle groups at once, which makes it realistic to hit all of these areas in a single session without spending hours in a gym.

Building a 2-day-a-week starting point

If you are brand new to strength training, here's a way to think about structuring two sessions that together cover the muscle groups listed by the CDC:

  • Session A: a lower-body-focused movement (works legs and hips), a pushing movement (works chest and shoulders), and a core movement (works abdomen).
  • Session B: a hip-hinge or back-focused movement (works back and hips), a pulling movement (works back and arms), and another core movement.

This is a general structure, not a prescription, since exact exercise selection depends on your equipment, any physical limitations, and personal preference. The key point from the guidelines is frequency and muscle group coverage, not a specific set of exercises.

Rest days matter as much as training days

Because the guideline is a minimum of 2 days, not a maximum, it leaves open the question of rest. Working the same muscle groups on consecutive days without recovery time is generally discouraged in most strength training guidance, which is why many beginner programs space out their 2 required sessions with at least one rest or light-activity day in between, rather than scheduling them back to back.

When 2 days a week isn't enough for your goals

The 2-day minimum is a public health floor meant to capture meaningful benefit for the general population, not necessarily the ideal frequency for someone training toward a specific performance or physique goal. If your goals go beyond general health, more frequent training may be appropriate, but that's a different question from what's needed to meet baseline health guidance. Starting at the minimum and building consistency first, then adding frequency later if your goals call for it, tends to be a more sustainable approach than starting ambitious and dropping off entirely.

Key takeaways

  • WHO and CDC guidance both set a minimum of at least 2 days per week of muscle-strengthening activity for adults.
  • This recommendation covers all major muscle groups: legs, hips, back, abdomen, chest, shoulders, and arms.
  • Compound exercises can cover multiple muscle groups per session, making a 2-day week realistic without long gym sessions.
  • The 2-day figure is a floor for general health benefit, not necessarily the right frequency for specific performance goals.
  • Spacing sessions apart, rather than scheduling them on consecutive days, is a common and sensible way to allow recovery.

Always consult a doctor before beginning a new exercise routine, particularly if you have a pre-existing health condition.
