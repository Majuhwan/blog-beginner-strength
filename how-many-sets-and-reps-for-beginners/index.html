How Many Sets and Reps Should a Beginner Do?

What the Physical Activity Guidelines say about sets and reps: why one set of 8 to 12 works, when 2 or 3 sets help, and how to tell if the weight is right.

Beginner StrengthOct 11, 2026 · 4 min read

"Three sets of ten" is the default answer at many gyms, but where does it come from, and is it the right starting point for a beginner? The federal Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans give a more specific answer than most people expect, and the National Institute on Aging (NIA) repeats it in its own exercise guidance. Here is what they say and how to use it.

First, the vocabulary

The Physical Activity Guidelines describe muscle-strengthening activity as having three components:

Component What it means, per the guidelines
Intensity How much weight or force is used relative to how much you are able to lift
Frequency How often you do muscle-strengthening activity
Sets and repetitions How many times you do the movement, like lifting a weight or doing a push-up

A repetition, or rep, is one complete movement: one push-up, one squat, one lift of a dumbbell. A set is a group of reps done in a row before you rest. The guidelines note that sets and reps play the same role for strength training that duration plays for aerobic activity.

The official number: one set of 8 to 12

Here is the core guidance, which appears in both the guidelines and NIA's advice:

  • One set of 8 to 12 repetitions of each exercise is effective.
  • Two or three sets may be more effective.

That is a useful message for beginners. You do not need three sets to get started. A single, well-chosen set per exercise already counts as an effective dose, according to the guidelines.

The "one more rep" test

Reps only work if the effort is real. The guidelines say there is no specific amount of time recommended for muscle strengthening, but that the exercises should be performed to the point at which it would be difficult to do another repetition. NIA adds that the exercises should involve at least a moderate level of intensity or effort.

Putting the two numbers together gives a practical way to choose a weight or band:

  • If you can finish 12 reps easily and could clearly do several more, the resistance is probably too light to meet the "difficult to do another" standard.
  • If you cannot reach 8 reps with good form, the resistance is probably too heavy for this rep range.
  • If reps 8 to 12 get hard, and the last one is a real effort, you are in the range the guidelines describe.

What a beginner session can look like

Here is an example using bodyweight moves from the official example lists, which include squats, lunges, push-ups, and planks:

Exercise Sets Reps
Squats 1 8 to 12
Push-ups 1 8 to 12
Lunges (alternating legs, counting both) 1 8 to 12

With one set of 8 to 12 reps for each of these three exercises, a session totals 24 to 36 reps. Moving to 2 sets doubles that to 48 to 72, and 3 sets triples it to 72 to 108. The numbers are for illustration; the guidelines set the per-set range, not a session total.

Planks do not fit neatly into reps because you hold a position instead of moving. The guidelines' definition covers this: muscle-strengthening activity can mean muscles that work or hold against a force.

NIA's beginner advice also applies here: if you are new, try exercises without weights or resistance bands until you are comfortable with the movements.

When to add a second or third set

The guidelines say 2 or 3 sets may be more effective, not that they are required. A reasonable path for a beginner:

  1. Start with one set per exercise while learning the movements.
  2. Once one set feels manageable from session to session, add a second set.
  3. Later, consider a third set if your schedule and recovery allow.

This matches the guidelines' broader point that strength development is progressive over time. They name three ways to progress: gradually increasing the amount of weight, the number of sets or repetitions, or the number of days a week of exercise.

Why the guidelines give a range, not a single number

A fixed number like "exactly 10" would ignore how different exercises and people are. The guidelines instead pair a rep range with an effort standard. Ten easy reps do not meet it; eight hard ones can. As you get stronger, the same weight will start to feel easy before rep 12, which is the signal to progress using one of the three levers above.

Key takeaways

  • The guidelines say one set of 8 to 12 reps per exercise is effective, and 2 or 3 sets may be more effective.
  • Each set should be taken to the point where another rep would be difficult.
  • If 12 reps are easy, the resistance is likely too light; if you cannot reach 8, it is likely too heavy.
  • Beginners can start with one set and add sets over time.
  • Progress comes from gradually adding weight, sets or reps, or days, according to the guidelines.
